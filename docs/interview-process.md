# The Career Interview Process

Treat the first stage as an interview, not a resume-writing session. The objective is to recover details that a short resume cannot hold.

## Start with a resume

**Give your model of choice an existing resume before asking it to build your career inventory.** It is the fastest way to give the model a useful starting picture of your work history, role progression, technologies, projects, and the language you've used to describe your experience.

A resume is a starting point, not the source of truth. It is usually incomplete, compressed for a particular job, and may be years out of date. Use it to orient the interview—not to limit what gets captured.

A useful first instruction is:

> Read this resume as background. Use it to establish my timeline and identify the roles, projects, technologies, and claims we should explore. Interview me to fill gaps and verify details. Do not assume the resume is complete or that every claim is still accurate.

If you have several versions, provide the most complete and recent one first. Older resumes can help recover earlier roles or projects that disappeared from newer versions. You can also provide permitted project notes, role descriptions, and performance-review excerpts. Remove confidential, customer, proprietary, or regulated information first. Do not upload employer secrets.

## Interview one role or project at a time

For each role, establish:

- Employer, business unit, title, dates, and progression.
- Products, systems, users, and business purpose.
- Your actual responsibilities and decision authority.
- Team structure, collaborators, vendors, and stakeholders.
- Architecture, technologies, integrations, and operational environment.
- Difficult problems, tradeoffs, incidents, and constraints.
- Outcomes, measurable results, and how results were measured.
- What you personally did versus what the team or another group did.

## Ask follow-up questions

- What did you personally implement, and what did another team own?
- Who made the final architecture decision? What influence did you have?
- What was different before your work?
- How do you know the result improved?
- Is that number measured, estimated, or remembered?
- What would a skeptical interviewer challenge?
- What details would help you explain this project on a whiteboard?
- What should we explicitly avoid claiming?

Do not accept vague statements such as "led modernization" without unpacking whether that meant technical direction, delivery coordination, architecture, people management, implementation, or a combination.

## Suggested interview cadence

1. Career timeline and role transitions.
2. Deep dive into the most recent role.
3. Earlier roles and major projects.
4. Architecture and technical decisions.
5. Leadership, influence, and vendor relationships.
6. Outcomes, metrics, and production responsibility.
7. Gaps, contradictions, and final fact-check.

Do not force this into one sitting. A careful multi-session interview is better than a rushed biography.

## Stop and validate

Review dates, metrics and denominators, team size, personal contribution, technology depth, leadership authority, certifications, and confidentiality. Preserve uncertainty in the private inventory with markers such as `VERIFY` or `NEEDS DETAIL`. These are reminders, not resume content.
